# Shatranj — الشطرنج (Shaṭranj)

**Summary.** Shatranj is the chess of the medieval Islamic world, played on an 8×8 board from Sasanian Persia (as *chatrang*) through the Abbasid court and al-Andalus to medieval Europe.
Its pieces are weaker than modern chess pieces: the counsellor (*firzān*) steps one square diagonally and the elephant (*fīl*) leaps exactly two squares diagonally.
A game is won by checkmate (*shāh māt*), by stalemating the opponent, or by leaving the opponent with a bare king. The bare-king rule has a counter-baring exception.

## 1. History

### 1.1 Origins: chaturanga → chatrang → shatranj
- The Arabic word *shaṭranj* (شطرنج) comes from Middle Persian *chatrang*, which comes from Sanskrit *chaturaṅga*, "four-limbed", meaning the four arms of an Indian army: elephants, horses, chariots and foot soldiers [4][6][8]. **[V]**
- The Middle Persian text *Wizārišn ī čatrang ud nihišn ī nēw-ardaxšīr* ("Explanation of chess and arrangement of backgammon") tells how an Indian king sent a chess set to the Sasanian king Khosrow I Anushirvan as a test, and the sage Wuzurgmihr (Bozorgmehr) worked the game out [5]. Iranica dates its core to the late Sasanian period; the surviving manuscript is late. **[V]**
- Iranica's account of the Pahlavi text gives the Persian piece names: *šāh* (king), *fračīn/farzīn* (counsellor), *pīl* (elephant), *asp* (horse), *rax/rukh* (rook) and *payādag/padātak* (foot soldier) [4]. **[V]**
- The *Kārnāmag ī Ardaxšīr ī Pābagān* credits Ardashir I with skill at *chatrang*. Scholars read this as evidence of the game's popularity when the text was written (late Sasanian), not in Ardashir's time [14]. **[U]** (weak source)
- After the Muslim conquest of Persia (7th century), Arabic speakers took the game over as *shaṭranj*. The Arabic piece names are Arabised forms of the Persian ones (see §3) [4][8]. **[V]**

### 1.2 The Abbasid golden age
- Chess was popular at the Abbasid court in Baghdad and Samarra. Popular accounts link Hārūn al-Rashīd (r. 786–809) with patronage of the game [16]. **[U]** (popular sources only)
- **al-ʿAdlī (العدلي)**, 9th century, wrote the first known treatise on shatranj. It is lost, but later compilations quote it. He discussed openings (ta'biyat) and composed problems (mansubat), and he ranked players into classes [8][10][17]. Sources disagree on his book's date: c. 800 or c. 842. **[V]** that the book existed and is lost; **[U]** its exact date.
- **ar-Rāzī (الرازي)**, Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn ʿUbayd Allāh ar-Rāzī (not the physician), beat al-ʿAdlī in front of Caliph **al-Mutawakkil** (r. 847–861). C.H.O'D. Alexander dates the match to 847 [8]. **[U]** The date comes from a later popular retelling; no primary account was found.
- **aṣ-Ṣūlī (الصولي)**, Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn Yaḥyā aṣ-Ṣūlī, born c. 870–880, died c. 946 in Basra. He was a court companion (*nadīm*) of the caliphs al-Muktafī, al-Muqtadir and ar-Rāḍī, and also a scholar and poet. He beat the earlier master al-Māwardī before al-Muktafī. His *Kitāb al-Shaṭranj* survives in part [8][16]. **[V]**
  - He correctly judged King+2 Knights vs King+Knight a win, where al-ʿAdlī had called it a draw. Modern endgame tablebases (Müller) confirm aṣ-Ṣūlī [8]. **[U]**
  - His "diamond" mansūba stayed unsolved for over a thousand years [8][16]. **[V]**
- **al-Lajlāj (اللجلاج)**, Abu'l-Faraj, 10th century, was reportedly aṣ-Ṣūlī's pupil and wrote a treatise on openings [16][13]. **[U]**
- A mid-12th-century compilation preserves extracts of al-ʿAdlī, aṣ-Ṣūlī and others. It was published in facsimile as *Kitāb al-Shaṭranj: selected texts from al-ʿAdlī, Abū Bakr aṣ-Ṣūlī and others* (Frankfurt, 1986) [15]. **[V]**
- Ibn al-Nadīm's *Fihrist* (377 AH / 988 CE) is the earliest bibliography to list books on chess (search excerpt; not checked in the edition). **[U]**

### 1.3 Material culture
- The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds a nearly complete 12th-century glazed stonepaste chess set from Iran (accession 1971.193a–ff), one of the earliest surviving sets. Its pieces are **abstract**, as was normal in the Islamic world: the shah is a throne shape, the fīl has two tusk-like knobs, the faras has a triangular head-knob, the rukh is a block with a cleft wedge, and the baidaq is a small faceted dome [6]. The Met also holds pawns excavated at Nishapur (9th–12th century) [6]. **[V]**
- Design note for the brand team: these abstract Islamic-period forms are a legitimate, historically grounded basis for Liwan's piece art, and they fit hard rule 4.

### 1.4 Spread to Europe
- Shatranj reached Christian Europe mainly through al-Andalus (Muslim Iberia) and also through Sicily and Byzantium, around the 10th–11th centuries [12][8]. **[V]** for the Iberian route; **[U]** for dates.
- The Latin *Versus de scachis*, also called the Einsiedeln poem (late 10th century), is one of the earliest European chess texts [8]. **[U]**
- **Alfonso X of Castile's *Libro de los juegos* (1283)** describes the rules, the pieces and 103 problems, many of them taken from Arabic mansubat [12]. **[V]**
- In Europe the *firzān* became the queen and the *fīl* became the bishop (Spanish *alfil* keeps the Arabic *al-fīl*). The modern queen and bishop moves appeared in the late 15th century [8]. **[V]**

### 1.5 Mansubat (المنصوبات, sing. منصوبة *manṣūba*)
- *Manṣūba* means "set-up position". Mansubat are composed positions with a stated goal, usually a forced win, and they are the ancestors of the modern chess problem and study. Hundreds survive in Arabic and Persian manuscripts, and Murray reproduced many of them [1][11][17]. **[V]**
- The best-known is **Dilārām's problem**. In the story, a player has staked his favourite, Dilārām, on the game. She tells him to sacrifice both rooks, and he mates. It is attributed to aṣ-Ṣūlī's circle or to al-ʿAdlī's book, and it is preserved in manuscripts dated before c. 946 [11][8]. **[U]** (the attribution varies)
- Some manuscript solutions are wrong, as later analysis found [11]. The product must verify every mansuba with the engine before shipping it as a puzzle.

## 2. Board

- **B1.** The board is 8×8, 64 squares, the same geometry as modern chess [1][4][8]. **[V]**
- **B2.** Medieval Islamic boards were usually **unchequered**: a plain grid, often on cloth or leather [1][8]. **[U]** Square colour plays no part in the rules (the fīl never changes square colour anyway). The product may draw an unchequered grid as the historical default, with an optional chequered "modern" skin.
- **B3. Orientation.** No rule about orientation ("light square on the right") is attested for shatranj. **[U]** (none found)
- **B4. Internal notation (engine).** Files `a`–`h` run from the first player's left to right and ranks `1`–`8` from the first player's side. Squares use algebraic names (`e1`). Each side's home rank is 1 or 8 and its pawn rank is 2 or 7. The engine always uses this notation, whatever the UI direction.
- **B5. Displayed notation (UI, RTL).** Arabic display may use the Arabic letters أ ب ج د هـ و ز ح for files a–h and Arabic-Indic digits for ranks. When the board is drawn RTL it is **mirrored visually only**: the model and the notation do not change. This is a product decision, not a historical one, and it is logged here so the UI and engine teams use the same mapping.

**Fīl note.** The passage in which Murray quotes al-ʿAdlī says that in the *Indian* game the elephant started in the corners and moved differently [10]. That describes the ancestor game, **not** shatranj. In shatranj the fīl stands between the faras and the firzān/shāh [1][10]. **[V]**

### 3.1 Starting position
- **SP1.** Rank 1 (first player) and rank 8 (second player), from file a to h: rukh, faras, fīl, *[shāh / firzān on d–e]*, fīl, faras, rukh. The baidaqs fill ranks 2 and 7. **[V]**
- **SP2.** The **placement of the shāh and firzān on the two central files was not fixed**. The FIDE Open Chess Museum says the white shāh's position, right or left, "was not fixed" [7]. Secondary sources say the shāh and firzān were often swapped [8]. **[V]** that it varied. Whether **the two kings faced each other** (same file) or **each king faced the enemy firzān** (mirrored) is also disputed:
  - Same file: one modern rules summary says the kings may start on either side of the centre but must be on the same file [18]. **[U]**
  - Mirrored ("each king faces the opposing counsellor"): Wikipedia's *Indian chess* article says this was unchanged in chatrang and shatranj [8]. **[U]**
  - See decision D2.
- **Default for the engine (pending D2):** shāh on **e1/e8**, firzān on **d1/d8**. This is the "same file" option and the arrangement most modern reconstructions use (WinBoard/xboard, Jocly, ChessV).

## 4. Full rules

Every rule has a number (R*n*) so that code and tests can cite it. Default choices that depend on a pending decision are marked *(D#)*.

### Turn and moves
- **R1.** Two players alternate turns, and each turn is exactly one move. Passing is not allowed. [1][8] **[V]**
- **R2.** First mover *(D4)*: the product default is that the player at the bottom ("light") moves first. The history is unclear: red (as in the Indian tradition) is claimed to have moved first, but on weak evidence [8], and one source says it did not matter [18]. **[U]**
- **R3.** A move is legal if it follows the piece's movement in §3 and lands on an empty square or on an enemy piece (a capture). A piece may never land on a friendly piece.
- **R4.** **Capture** is by displacement: the captured piece leaves the board. Every piece captures the way it moves, except the baidaq, which captures diagonally forward (§3).
- **R5.** The rukh slides and cannot pass over pieces. The fīl and the faras jump, and the squares between do not matter. The shāh, firzān and baidaq move a single step.
- **R6.** **No double baidaq step** on any move [1][8][18]. **[V]**
- **R7.** **No en passant**, because en passant only exists when a double step exists [8]. **[V]**
- **R8.** **No castling** [1][8]. **[V]** (Castling is a later European invention.)
- **R9.** **Promotion.** A baidaq that reaches the far rank (rank 8 for the first player, rank 1 for the second) **becomes a firzān** at once, as part of the same move. No other choice is allowed [4][8][18]. **[V]** Iranica gives the Persian term *farzīn-šodan* ("becoming a firzān") [4].
  - **R9a.** Promotion happens even if the side already has a firzān. More than one firzān per side is legal. **[V]** (it follows from R9, and multi-firzān positions appear in mansubat)
  - **R9b.** A promoted firzān moves like any firzān. Some later European rules gave a newly promoted ferz a special first leap; that is **not** attested for shatranj and is **not** implemented. **[U]** (not found in shatranj sources)
- **R10.** A baidaq on the last rank cannot exist unpromoted, because R9 always applies.

### Check and mate
- **R11.** **Check (shāh, شاه):** a shāh is in check when an enemy piece could capture it on the next move. The custom was to call "*shāh*" out loud [1]. **[U]** The app shows check visually and may play an optional voice line "شاه!".
- **R12.** A player may not make a move that leaves their own shāh in check, and that includes moving the shāh next to the enemy shāh. Every source and reconstruction treats the game this way. **[V]** (No source was found for a medieval "king capture" variant.)
- **R13.** **Checkmate (shāh māt, شاه مات, "the king is helpless/defeated"):** the side to move is in check and has no legal move. **The checking side wins.** [1][8] **[V]**

### Stalemate *(D1)*
- **R14.** **Stalemate:** the side to move is **not** in check and has no legal move. Default (classical shatranj): **the player who delivered the stalemate wins** [8][9][1]. **[V]** for classical Arabic shatranj, from several independent secondary sources that cite Murray.
  - The **Indian** game as reported by al-ʿAdlī, in Murray's quotation, used the opposite rule: the stalemated player won [10]. That is a different, earlier rule set.
  - The modern rule (stalemate is a draw) is **not** historical for shatranj.

### Bare king (al-ʿarī / "baring") *(D3)*
- **R15.** A side's shāh is **bare** when that side has no men left except the shāh. Baidaqs count as men. [8][18] **[V]**
- **R16.** **Win by baring.** If a player captures the opponent's last non-king man, that player wins, **subject to R17** [8][18]. **[V]**
- **R17.** **Counter-baring (default):** after a baring move the bared side gets **one more move**. If the bared shāh can then capture the opponent's **last** non-king man, which bares the opponent in turn, the game is a **draw** (shāh against shāh). If it cannot, or does not do so, the baring side wins [8][18]. **[V]** (several secondary sources agree; the original-text wording was not seen)
  - **R17a.** **Medinese (Hijazi) variant:** in Medina the player who bared first **won even if** the opponent could bare back at once. Some sources call this the "Medinese victory" [8][10]. **[V]** that the variant existed. Murray quotes al-ʿAdlī saying the Indian game had the same "first baring wins" rule [10].
  - **R17b.** Engine detail: counter-baring is only possible when the baring side has **exactly one** non-king man left after the baring move, and that man can be captured by the bared shāh on the very next move. The capture must also be legal under R12: the shāh may not capture a protected piece, but in this case nothing can protect it except the opponent's shāh.
- **R18.** **Priority on the same move.** If one move both mates and bares, it counts as mate (R13). If it bares and also leaves the opponent with no legal move (stalemate), the result is a win for the baring player either way: under R14 by stalemate and under R16 by baring, and the counter-bare cannot happen because the bared side has no move. **[U]** (this follows from R13–R17; no explicit source was found)
- **R19.** If the baring move also gives check, the bared side must answer the check like any other check (R12). If it can do so by capturing the checking man, and that man is the opponent's last man, that capture is a counter-bare (R17). If it has no legal move, it is mated (R13).

### Draws *(D5)*
- **R20.** **Shāh against shāh** (both bare, after a counter-bare under R17) is a draw [18]. **[V]**
- **R21.** **Draw by agreement** is allowed. This is the product default; draws by agreement are implied by the historical classing of drawn endgames, but no explicit rule text was found. **[U]**
- **R22.** **Repetition / long games: no repetition rule or "N-move" rule is attested for shatranj.** **[U]** (searched for and not found) One modern secondary page says an old perpetual-check-by-declaration draw existed, but it could not be verified. An app needs termination anyway, so the product default is the **house rules** below, which are **named as house rules** in the UI:
  - **R22a (house).** Threefold repetition of the same position, with the same side to move, is a draw.
  - **R22b (house).** 70 consecutive moves by each side (140 plies) with no capture and no baidaq move is a draw. This is longer than modern chess's 50 moves because shatranj endgames are slow, and the D5 options below cover the alternatives.
- **R23.** Endgames such as firzān vs firzān are **not** declared drawn automatically. Al-ʿAdlī and aṣ-Ṣūlī classified endings as won or drawn by analysis, not by rule [8], so the game continues until R13–R22 decide it.

## 5. Opening setups — ta'biyāt (التعبيات, sing. تعبية *taʿbiya*)

- Strong players often began from named **formations** (*taʿbiya*, "battle array"). Each side reached its formation in roughly 10–20 moves, often while almost ignoring the opponent's play, because pieces are so slow that early contact is rare. Al-ʿAdlī described them first, and aṣ-Ṣūlī and al-Lajlāj discussed them later [10][13][17]. **[V]** that they existed and were discussed.
- Sources disagree on how many there were (14, 16, or about 31) and on which ones aṣ-Ṣūlī kept [13][8]. **[U]**
- Named examples that appear in several secondary sources [13][8]. **[U]** for the exact move sequences, none of which were checked against Murray:
  1. **al-Mujannaḥ (المجنّح, "the winged"):** a flank formation. Al-Lajlāj reportedly said it is the one ta'biya in which every piece is developed within twelve moves.
  2. **al-Mashāʾikhī (المشائخي, "of the masters"):** a slower, central formation that aṣ-Ṣūlī reportedly ranked highly.
  3. **as-Sayyāl (السيّال, "the flowing"):** a quick king-side baidaq advance.
- **Product use (optional mode, later step):** offer a "Start from a ta'biya" mode in which the board is set up in a named formation and play begins from there. **The formations' exact squares must be taken from Murray [1] or a scholarly edition before shipping.** Until then, ship no ta'biya positions.

## 7. Notes for implementation

### 7.1 Engine requirements
- The state holds: the board, the side to move, a ply counter for R22b (no capture and no baidaq move), a position-history hash for R22a, an optional `pendingBare` flag (R17: the side that has just been bared gets one reply), and a variant config (`stalemate`, `counterBare`, `setup`, `firstMover`, `drawRules`).
- `result()` is evaluated after every applied move, in this order:
  1. The side to move has no legal moves: in check → checkmate (R13); not in check → stalemate (R14/D1).
  2. Baring check (R15–R17):
     - If the side to move is bare and the opponent is not, then if `counterBare` is on and the bared shāh has a legal capture of the opponent's last man, the game continues for one move. Otherwise the opponent wins.
     - If the move just made was the counter-bare (both sides bare) → draw (R20).
     - If the bared side's single reply did not counter-bare → the baring side wins.
  3. House draws (R22).
- Hashing: no castling or en-passant state exists. The position key is the board plus the side to move, plus `pendingBare`.
- Move generation needs no "promotion piece" choice. A move to the last rank always promotes to firzān.
- The AI search (game_core) must treat stalemate and baring as **terminal wins** in its evaluation. That makes material and baidaq structure much more important than in modern chess.

### 7.2 Test positions
Positions use the engine FEN-style notation (K shāh, Q firzān, B fīl, N faras, R rukh, P baidaq; uppercase = first player, "w" = first player to move).

1. **Initial position** (`rnbqkbnr/pppppppp/8/8/8/8/PPPPPPPP/RNBQKBNR w - - 0 1`): first-player legal moves = 8 baidaq single steps + 4 faras (b1-a3, b1-c3, g1-f3, g1-h3) + 4 fīl leaps (c1-a3, c1-e3, f1-d3, f1-h3; each jumps over the occupied b2/d2/e2/g2) + 0 firzān + 0 shāh = **16**.
2. **Fīl jumps over pieces:** `4k3/8/8/8/8/8/1P6/B3K3 w - - 0 1` → a1-c3 is legal although b2 is occupied.
3. **Fīl reach:** from c1 a fīl can only ever reach the 8 squares {a3, a7, c1, c5, e3, e7, g1, g5}. Test the reachable set on an empty board.
4. **Firzān step:** a firzān on d4 on an otherwise empty board (shāhs on a1/h8) has exactly c3, e3, c5, e5.
5. **No double step:** a baidaq on e2 with e3 and e4 empty → only e2-e3.
6. **Promotion:** `8/4P3/8/8/8/8/k7/7K w - - 0 1` → e7-e8 produces a firzān on e8. No other choice exists. Repeat with an extra first-player firzān on the board; promotion is still legal (R9a).
7. **No castling:** `4k3/8/8/8/8/8/8/4K2R w - - 0 1` → no move puts the shāh on g1 or the rukh on f1 together.
8. **Checkmate:** `R6k/p7/6K1/8/8/8/8/8 b - - 0 1` → black is in check from a8, g8/g7/h7 are covered, and the a7-a6 baidaq move does not help → **checkmate, first player wins (R13)**.
9. **Stalemate:** `k7/p1K5/P7/8/8/8/8/8 b - - 0 1` → black shāh a8 is not in check; b8 and b7 are covered by the shāh on c7 (b7 also by the baidaq on a6); the black baidaq a7 is blocked by a6 and has nothing to capture on b6 → **stalemate**. Expected: D1-A first player wins; D1-B second player wins; D1-C draw.
10. **Bare king, no counter-bare:** `4k3/8/8/n7/8/8/8/R3K3 w - - 0 1` → 1. Ra1xa5 bares black, and black cannot reach a5 → after any black reply, **first player wins by baring (R16)**.
11. **Counter-bare draw:** `4k3/3p4/8/8/8/8/8/K2R4 w - - 0 1` → 1. Rd1xd7 bares black. The rukh on d7 is unprotected, so black plays Ke8xd7 → both shāhs bare → **draw (R17, R20)**. Under the Medina toggle (D3-B) the result is a **first-player win** right after 1. Rxd7. If black does not play Kxd7 (e.g. Kf8), first player wins.
12. **Protected last man (no counter-bare):** `2K1k3/3p4/8/8/8/8/8/3R4 w - - 0 1` → 1. Rxd7. Kxd7 is illegal because the shāh on c8 guards d7 (R12, R17b). Black must play Kf8 → **first player wins**.
13. **Bared side in check, counter-bares by capturing the checker:** `4k3/4p3/8/8/8/8/8/4R2K w - - 0 1` → 1. Re1xe7+ bares black and gives check. 1... Kxe7 is legal (the rukh is unprotected) → **draw**. It must not be scored as mate (R19).
14. **Mate beats baring:** `n6k/8/6K1/8/8/8/8/R7 w - - 0 1` → 1. Ra1xa8 captures black's last man **and** mates → result reason = **checkmate** (R18), not bare.
15. **Threefold repetition (house rule R22a):** shāh/rukh shuffles → draw on the 3rd occurrence of the same position with the same side to move.
16. **70-move house rule (R22b):** the counter resets on any capture and any baidaq move (including promotion), and the draw comes at 140 plies without either.
17. **Mirrored setup (D2-C):** the initial position has shāh e1 and shāh d8, and the move count is still 16 per side.

## 8. Notes for tutorials (suggested learning order)

1. **The board and the armies.** An 8×8 unchequered board, the six pieces with their Arabic names, and a short story frame (Wuzurgmihr and the Indian king's challenge).
2. **The rukh.** The strongest piece. Slides in straight lines; capturing.
3. **The faras.** The L-jump, which works the same as in modern chess, so families can carry it over.
4. **The fīl.** The 2-square diagonal leap that jumps over pieces, with the "only 8 squares" puzzle as a mini-game.
5. **The firzān.** One diagonal step: the king's counsellor, not a "queen".
6. **The baidaq.** One step forward, diagonal capture, no double step, and promotion to a new firzān.
7. **The shāh and check.** Saying "shāh!", escaping check, and why kings cannot stand next to each other.
8. **Shāh māt.** Simple mates with a rukh and with two rukhs. This is the first chance to tell the Dilārām story as a puzzle.
9. **The other ways to win.** Stalemate wins (classical rule) and baring the king, plus the counter-bare draw. This is where shatranj most clearly differs from modern chess.
10. **Thinking like the masters.** A slow middlegame, the idea of ta'biyat, and a short story about aṣ-Ṣūlī at the caliph's court, ending with a first mansuba challenge.
